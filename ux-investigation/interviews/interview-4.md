# Interview: Erik V., 26
*CS Student & OSS Contributor, Gothenburg*

**Q1:** Walk me through the last time you had trouble with measurements on Amazon. Step by step?

**A1:** Yeah, so I was looking at a standing desk on Amazon US — I follow r/battlestations and someone linked a specific model. I click through, and it says the desktop is 60 by 24 inches. Okay, I know roughly what an inch is, but I need the exact centimeters to know if it fits my apartment niche. So I open a terminal, type `python3 -c "print(60 * 2.54, 24 * 2.54)"` and get my numbers. The annoying part isn't the conversion itself — it's that I had to context-switch out of the browser, and then I had to do it again for the height range, and again for the weight capacity in pounds. Four separate terminal invocations for one product page. It's not painful, it's just... friction. And honestly the worst part was that the product had measurements scattered across the title, the bullet points, AND some weird specs table at the bottom, all in different formats. Some had the double-quote symbol for inches, some spelled it out.

**Q2:** What's the most frustrating part of how you handle unit conversion today?

**A2:** It's not that my method is bad — `python3 -c` is fast and accurate, and I trust the output. The frustrating part is that it's manual and it doesn't scale. If I'm comparing three monitors side by side, I'm running like twelve conversions. Copy value, switch to terminal, type the expression, read the result, switch back, find where I was on the page. It breaks my flow. And sometimes I'll fat-finger a number and get a wrong result and not notice until I've already mentally committed to a product. There's no inline visual — I have to hold the converted numbers in my head while scrolling. But honestly? I'd rather have that friction than install something that corrupts the page or gives me wrong numbers. At least with Python I know the math is right.

**Q3:** What have you tried before? Why did it stop working?

**A3:** Oh, I've been through the gauntlet. First was Everything Metric — seemed popular, decent GitHub activity. But it was triggering on things that weren't measurements. Product codes, model numbers, random strings with numbers and letters. It converted a "12V power supply" description into some metric nonsense. Uninstalled. Then I tried autoConvert, and that one literally took a timestamp — "2h24m30s" in a video description — and converted it into centimeters. That's when I realized these tools are just running regex patterns with no semantic understanding of what they're matching. I also tried Imperial to Metric by esprimo, which was less aggressive but still had edge cases, and Metrication, which replaced the original text entirely so you couldn't even see what the imperial value was. That's a cardinal sin — you should never destroy the source data. After four failed attempts I just went back to my terminal and accepted the friction. At this point my bar for trying a fifth extension is extremely high.

**Q4:** If a new Chrome extension claims to auto-convert measurements on Amazon — what builds trust? What makes you close the tab?

**A4:** First thing: I'm reading the source code. If it's not open source, I'm already gone. Second, I check the GitHub — when was the last commit? Are there tests? What's the test coverage? If I see zero tests on a tool that does pattern matching on arbitrary web content, that tells me the developer doesn't take correctness seriously. I also look at open issues — specifically for false positive reports. If there are multiple "it converted something that wasn't a measurement" issues, I know exactly what I'm getting into. What makes me close the tab instantly: Manifest V2, no source available, permissions that are too broad — like requesting access to all URLs when it should only need Amazon. Also, any marketing language like "AI-powered" or "smart conversion" without explaining the actual algorithm. And honestly? If it's a Chrome extension, I probably won't use it anyway because I use Firefox. But I know Chrome extensions can sometimes work in Firefox with minor tweaks, so I'd at least look.

**Q5:** What would you pay for this? What are you comparing it to?

**A5:** Zero. I wouldn't pay for a unit converter. Not because the problem isn't real, but because the solution space is trivial. Converting inches to centimeters is multiplication by 2.54 — I'm not paying someone to multiply for me. What I'd pay for is *accuracy and zero false positives*, but no one's proven they can deliver that yet. If someone did, I'd maybe throw five euros at it as a one-time purchase, but definitely not a subscription. I'm comparing it to free Python, free terminal, free `units` command on Linux. The value proposition isn't the math — it's the UX of inline conversion without leaving the page. And that UX has to be flawless. One false positive and I'm back to the terminal, which costs me nothing. So the real competitor isn't other extensions — it's my existing workflow plus my low tolerance for broken tools.

**Q6:** Who else in your life deals with this? How differently?

**A6:** My roommate Axel orders from Amazon US constantly — keyboards, audio gear, random gadgets. He just... doesn't convert. He's developed this weird intuitive sense where he "feels" what 60 inches is. Sometimes he's wildly wrong and a package arrives and it doesn't fit, but he doesn't seem bothered by it. My mom asks me to convert things for her when she's shopping — she'll text me screenshots of product pages and I'll text back the metric values. She'd probably benefit most from an extension like this, but she also wouldn't know how to install one, and I wouldn't install something on her browser that I don't trust myself. My girlfriend uses Google — she literally types "48 inches in cm" into the search bar every single time. It works, but it's the same context-switching problem I have, just with more steps.

**Q7:** Show me your phone — any related apps or tools?

**A7:** I don't have a unit conversion app on my phone. I have the Python interpreter — Pythonista — which I occasionally use for quick calculations, but that's a general tool, not a converter. On my laptop, which is more relevant, I have a shell alias: `alias ic='python3 -c "import sys; print(float(sys.argv[1]) * 2.54)"'` so I can just type `ic 48` and get 121.92. That's the closest thing to a "tool" I have for this. I also have a Firefox bookmark folder called "US shopping" where I keep links to Amazon US, and I've thought about writing a Tampermonkey script to do inline conversion, but I've never actually done it because the problem isn't annoying enough to justify the maintenance burden. The irony is that I could probably build a better converter than any of the ones I've tried, but the effort-to-benefit ratio doesn't justify it... yet.

**Q8:** What feature would you love that nobody's built?

**A8:** Semantic awareness. Every converter I've seen is just regex — they match patterns like `\d+\s*(inches|in|"|lbs|oz)` and convert blindly. What I want is something that understands context. It should know that "12V" is not twelve inches. It should know that "Model 4500" is not a measurement. It should know that "2h24m30s" is a duration, not a length. Basically, I want the converter to have zero false positives, and I'd accept missing some real measurements in exchange. False negatives are fine — I can convert those manually. False positives are unforgivable because they make me distrust the entire tool. I'd also love a confidence indicator — like, show me the conversion but with a subtle visual cue of how confident the tool is that this is actually a measurement. That way I can glance and know which conversions to trust and which to double-check.

**Q9:** What would make you recommend this to a friend?

**A9:** Honestly? It would have to survive a month on my browser without a single false positive. That's my bar. If I install it and use it daily for a month and it never converts something that isn't a measurement, never corrupts a page layout, never adds more than 50 milliseconds to page load, and the conversions are always correct — then I'd recommend it. I'd also need to see active maintenance on the repo. If the developer stops pushing commits, I'm assuming the extension is dead and I'm uninstalling it before it becomes a security liability. I'd recommend it to my mom's setup and my girlfriend before I'd recommend it to my programmer friends, because my programmer friends all have their own terminal workflows and the same skepticism I do. But for normal people who just Google every conversion? Yeah, a working extension would be genuinely useful for them.

**Q10:** What am I not asking that I should be?

**A10:** You're not asking about performance. Every extension I've tried adds noticeable page load time because they're scanning the entire DOM on every page. Amazon pages are massive — thousands of elements, lazy-loaded content, dynamic updates. A naive converter that walks the DOM looking for measurement patterns is going to be slow, and it's going to miss content that loads after the initial scan. You should be asking: how does this handle dynamically loaded content? Does it use a MutationObserver? What's the performance impact on a product comparison page with 50+ items? Also, you're not asking about privacy. An extension that reads page content has access to everything on that page — my browsing behavior, what I'm shopping for, prices, my cart contents. What data does it collect? Does it phone home? Does it have a privacy policy? These are dealbreakers for me, and they should be dealbreakers for anyone installing browser extensions in 2026.
